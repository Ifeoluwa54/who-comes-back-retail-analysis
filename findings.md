# Findings: "Who Comes Back?"

Source: UCI Online Retail II, analysis window Dec 2009 to Nov 2011. All figures are gross of returns, in pounds sterling. Strength labels: **Strong** = large samples and a clear gap. **Moderate** = a real pattern but with limits. **Tentative** = a lead to investigate, not a conclusion.

## 1. Most new customers come back, and quickly (Strong)

Among 3,970 new customers with at least 180 days of history, 15.9% placed a second order within 30 days, 40.1% within 90 days and 56.4% within 180 days. Among customers who did return, the median wait was 68 days.
*For a retailer:* the first three months decide whether a customer becomes a repeat buyer, so follow-up should happen in that window.
*Caution:* this is a wholesale-heavy retailer, so repeat rates are higher than a typical consumer shop.

## 2. Bigger first orders go with much more repeat buying and revenue (Strong)

| First order size | Range | 180-day repeat rate | Average 365-day revenue | Median 365-day revenue |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Smallest quarter | up to £170 | 45.6% | £686 | £243 |
| Second | £170 to £299 | 54.4% | £937 | £554 |
| Third | £299 to £457 | 61.4% | £1,336 | £886 |
| Largest quarter | £457 and above | 64.5% | £3,299 | £1,942 |

*For a retailer:* the size of the first order is an early signal of a customer's value.
*Caution:* this is an association. Wholesale buyers both place large first orders and reorder, so size is partly a marker of customer type. Averages are much higher than medians, so a small number of very large buyers pull them up.

## 3. A small group of customers brings in most of the revenue (Strong)

The top 10% of customers account for 63.5% of revenue. By segment, Champions are 26.0% of customers and 73.0% of revenue, averaging £8,145 each and 13.7 order days.
*For a retailer:* losing a few top customers would hurt far more than losing many small ones.

## 4. About one in five of a cohort keeps ordering, with little decline over time (Moderate)

Weighted across cohorts, 21.0% of customers ordered in month 1, 21.1% in month 3, 18.4% in month 6 and 18.4% in month 12. The curve is flat rather than steadily falling, which suggests a loyal core that keeps buying while the rest drop away.
*Limits:* month-12 figures rest on 11 cohorts, individual cohorts vary a lot (for example 2010-01 had 31.5% in month 3, 2010-11 had 9.5%), and calendar-month retention understates customers who order every couple of months.

## 5. There appears to be a seasonal pattern (Moderate for autumn, Tentative for December)

Cohorts that start in September and October show higher month-1 activity in both years: 23.0% and 25.9% for 2010, and 27.1% and 31.7% for 2011. The December 2010 cohort, by contrast, had only 9.2% in month 1 (76 customers).
*Limits:* the autumn pattern repeats across two years, but there are only four data points. The December result is a single small cohort. This is likely the gift trade building up before Christmas, but the data cannot prove it.

## 6. Some first-purchase products show high repeat rates (Tentative)

Among products with at least 50 first-time buyers, the top 15 have repeat rates of 74% to 80% against the 56.4% baseline. Themes in that list: ceramic drawer knobs (3 of the top 15), woodland designs (tissues, lunch bag, Charlotte bag) and kids' or baking items (aprons, cake cases). At the bottom, hand warmers make up 3 of the lowest 8, plus a Christmas food item.
*Limits:* groups are small (50 to 150 customers each), 607 products were compared so the extremes are partly luck, and products in big baskets are favoured because big first orders repeat more. A possible explanation is that everyday, reorderable items bring customers back while seasonal or one-off items do not, but this analysis cannot test that.

## 7. A win-back group is sitting in plain sight (Strong that it exists; the action is a suggestion)

The "At risk" segment has 667 customers (11.5%), who bought often (average 5.1 order days) and averaged £2,260 each, but whose last order was a median of 352 days ago. Together they brought in £1.5M (8.9% of revenue). By contrast, "Lost or low" is 1,713 customers (29.4%) but only 4.8% of revenue.
*For a retailer:* personal outreach to the At risk group is worth more than broad reactivation emails to the Lost or low group.

## What not to claim

- That any product "causes" repeat buying.
- That these rates apply to consumer retail or to today.
- Net revenue or profit figures (everything is gross of returns).
